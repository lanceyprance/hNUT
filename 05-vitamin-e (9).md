## Part A: Core questions

### Q1: Forms and sources (Core Q1)

Name the two families of vitamin E vitamers. Which is the most active form? Give three good food sources.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Tocopherols and tocotrienols (each as α, β, γ, δ). They cannot interconvert.

α-tocopherol is the most active.

Sources (any three): sunflower oil, safflower oil, almonds, hazelnuts, other nuts and seeds, vegetable oils, green leafy vegetables.

From: §1, §2

</details>

---

### Q2: Free radicals (Core Q2)

What is a free radical? Describe the free radical cascade and the damage it causes.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A free radical is a molecule with an unpaired electron, which makes it highly reactive.

It steals an electron from a stable molecule. That molecule becomes a new free radical and seeks another stable molecule: a chain reaction (cascade).

Radicals damage cell membranes, DNA, RNA and proteins. This affects transport in and out of cells, impairs cell function, and triggers an inflammatory response.

From: §4

</details>

---

### Q3: Antioxidant mechanism (Core Q3)

Explain how vitamin E acts as an antioxidant, and how it is regenerated.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Vitamin E limits radical damage from oxidation of PUFAs in cell membranes.

It donates a hydrogen (an electron) from its phenolic –OH group to the radical, neutralising it and ending the chain reaction.

Vitamin E becomes a tocopheroxyl radical, which is stable because the aromatic ring stabilises it (resonance).

Vitamin C reduces it back to tocopherol.

From: §5

</details>

### Q4: Calculation: α-tocopherol equivalents (Core Q4)

Per 100 g, an oil contains 30 mg α-tocopherol, 10 mg β-tocopherol, 40 mg γ-tocopherol and 5 mg α-tocotrienol.

Find its vitamin E content in mg α-TE.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: multiply each vitamer by its factor.**

α-tocopherol: 30 × 1 = 30

β-tocopherol: 10 × 0.5 = 5

γ-tocopherol: 40 × 0.1 = 4

α-tocotrienol: 5 × 0.3 = 1.5

**Step 2: add.**

α-TE = 30 + 5 + 4 + 1.5 = **40.5 mg α-TE per 100 g**

From: §2 Equation 1

</details>

### Q5: Evidence (Core Q5)

What do epidemiological studies and randomised controlled trials show about vitamin E and cardiovascular disease?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Epidemiological studies show a clear association between high blood vitamin E and lower incidence of CVD.

Randomised controlled trials with high-dose vitamin E supplements have been disappointing: small effect, no effect, or increased health risks.

From: §8

</details>

A person eats 20 g of PUFA a day.

1. Find their vitamin E Adequate Intake.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**1.**

AI = 0.4 × 20 = **8 mg α-TE/day**

**2.**

Tablespoons = 8 ÷ 5.6 = **1.4 tbsp**

### M5: Explain

Soybean oil has much more γ-tocopherol than sunflower oil, but less α-tocopherol. Why can't you compare their vitamin E just by adding up all the tocopherols?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Different vitamers have different biological potency. α-tocopherol is the most active.

γ-tocopherol counts only 0.1, β-tocopherol 0.5 and α-tocotrienol 0.3 of α-tocopherol.

So you have to convert to α-tocopherol equivalents first, multiplying each vitamer by its factor, before adding.

From: §2 Equation 1

</details>

---

### M6: Explain

Why does a diet high in PUFA increase the requirement for vitamin E?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Vitamin E's main job is to protect PUFAs in cell membranes from oxidation by free radicals.

More PUFA means more fat that can be oxidised, so more vitamin E is needed to protect it.

That's why the AI is set as 0.4 mg α-TE per g of dietary PUFA.

From: §2 Equation 2, §5

</details>
