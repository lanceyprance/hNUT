## The big idea

Vitamin B6 is the **protein vitamin**.
Its active form, **pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)**, is the coenzyme for 100+ reactions, mostly in **amino acid metabolism**: moving amino groups around (**transamination**) and making **neurotransmitters** like **GABA**.

So the more **protein** you eat, the more B6 you need. The requirement is set **per gram of protein**.

Most of your B6 actually sits in **muscle**, attached to **glycogen phosphorylase**, the enzyme that breaks down glycogen.

## ⭐ Learn by heart from this topic

**6 vitamers:** pyridox**ine** (alcohol), pyridox**al** (aldehyde), pyridox**amine** (amine), each with or without a phosphate.
**Active form: pyridoxal phosphate (PLP).** End product **4-pyridoxic acid** is inactive and excreted.

**Intake:** AR **13 µg per g protein**, reference intake **15 µg per g protein**. EFSA PRI **1.6–1.7 mg/day**.

**Metabolism:** absorbed **free** (dephosphorylated) → liver **phosphorylates** and **oxidises** to PLP → exported on **albumin**. **80%** of body B6 is in **muscle**, on **glycogen phosphorylase**.

**3 jobs:**
1. **Amino acid reactions**: **transamination** (non-essential amino acids) and **decarboxylation** (glutamate → **GABA**)
2. Cofactor of **glycogen phosphorylase** (glycogen breakdown)
3. **Regulation of steroid hormones** (switches off their action)

**Deficiency:** severe is **rare**. Moderate: mood changes, skin and mucosal problems, **mild anaemia**, reduced immunity. (Summary: dermatitis, irritability, anaemia.)

## 1. The 6 vitamers

![B6 vitamers](../img/10/l15-06.jpg)

**How to read this diagram:** all six share a **pyridine ring**. Only the group at the top (green boxes) changes.

| Vitamer | Group at the top | Type |
|---|---|---|
| **Pyridoxine** | –CH₂OH | **alcohol** |
| **Pyridoxal** | –CHO | **aldehyde** |
| **Pyridoxamine** | –CH₂NH₂ | **amine** |

Each can have a **phosphate** added (by a **kinase**). That gives the other three:
pyridoxine phosphate, **pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)**, pyridoxamine phosphate.

The other phosphates are **oxidised** to **PLP**, the active coenzyme (red box).

**4-pyridoxic acid** (bottom left) is the **biologically inactive endpoint** of B6 metabolism. It's **excreted** in urine.

<br>

**Memory hook:** pyridox**al** = **al**dehyde = the active one. **PLP** = **P**yridoxa**L** **P**hosphate.

### 📐 Equation 1: B6 from protein intake

**B6 requirement (µg/day) = 15 µg × protein intake (g/day)** (reference intake)

**Average requirement (µg/day) = 13 µg × protein intake (g/day)**

| Symbol | Means | Units |
|---|---|---|
| 15 | reference intake of B6 per g protein | µg/g |
| 13 | average requirement of B6 per g protein | µg/g |
| protein intake | how much protein is eaten | g/day |

**What it means:**
B6 is the coenzyme for amino acid metabolism (§4). More protein to process = more B6 needed.

**Use it when:**
You're given a protein intake and asked for the B6 need.

**Watch out:**
The answer comes out in **µg**. Divide by 1000 for **mg** (EFSA's PRI is 1.6–1.7 **mg**/day).

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

That's close to EFSA's PRI of 1.6–1.7 mg/day.

</details>

## 3. Absorption and metabolism

![B6 metabolism](../img/10/l15-08.jpg)

**How to read this diagram:** gut at the bottom, liver at the top, blood vessel on the right.

1. B6 is **absorbed in the small intestine** in its **free form** (**dephosphorylated**).
2. **Most is taken up by the liver.** There it's:
   - **phosphorylated** (phosphorylated forms **can't cross cell membranes**, so it's trapped)
   - **oxidised** to **pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)**
3. PLP is **exported bound to albumin** in the blood.
4. **Tissue levels** are controlled by the balance between **phosphorylation** (trap it in) and **dephosphorylation** (let it out).
5. **80%** of the body's B6 is in **muscle**, as PLP attached to **glycogen phosphorylase**.

<br>

**The phosphate is the lock:** take it off to get through membranes, put it on to keep it inside the cell.

### 🧠 Check
Where is most of the body's B6, and what is it doing there?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

About 80% is in muscle, as pyridoxal phosphate.

It's the coenzyme for glycogen phosphorylase, the enzyme that breaks down glycogen.

</details>

## 4. Function: 3 areas of metabolism

PLP is a coenzyme in **100+ reactions**, in **3 main areas**:

<br>

**1. Amino acid reactions**

**a. Transamination**
- PLP is the coenzyme for **transaminases**: it **carries the amino group**.
- It moves an amino group onto an **α-keto acid**.
- This is how the body makes **non-essential amino acids**.
- Example: **alanine + α-ketoglutarate → pyruvate + glutamate**

**b. Decarboxylation to form amines**
- Removing a carboxyl group from an amino acid gives an amine.
- Example: **glutamate → γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)**, a **neurotransmitter**

<br>

**2. Cofactor of glycogen phosphorylase**
- the enzyme that **breaks down glycogen** (stored glucose)
- that's the muscle B6 from §3

<br>

**3. Regulation of steroid hormones**
- B6 helps **terminate** the action of steroid hormones
- it **removes the hormone–receptor complex** from where it's bound to **DNA**

<br>

**Memory hook: "AGS"** → **A**mino acids, **G**lycogen, **S**teroids.

### 🧠 Check
Give the transamination example from the slides, and say what the amino group moves between.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Alanine + α-ketoglutarate → pyruvate + glutamate.

The amino group moves from alanine onto the α-keto acid (α-ketoglutarate), which becomes glutamate. PLP carries it.

</details>

## 5. Deficiency

**Severe deficiency is rare**, because B6 is **widely distributed** in food.

**Moderate deficiency** causes:
- **abnormal amino acid metabolism** (job 1 fails)
- **over-sensitivity to steroid hormones** (job 3 fails: hormones aren't switched off)

These lead to:
- **mood changes** (less GABA)
- **skin and mucosal** problems
- **mild anaemia**
- **reduced immune function**

Summary slide: **dermatitis, irritability, anaemia**.

<br>

**Link the cause to the symptom:** each symptom comes from one of the 3 jobs breaking down.

### 🧠 Check
Why might moderate B6 deficiency make someone more sensitive to steroid hormones?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

B6 normally terminates steroid hormone action by removing the hormone–receptor complex from DNA.

Without enough B6, the hormones keep acting for longer.

</details>
