## The big idea

Vitamin K is the **clotting** vitamin. "K" is from the German *Koagulation*.

It's a **cofactor** that adds a carboxyl group to **glutamate** in certain proteins.
That turns glutamate into **Gla**, which can grab **calcium**.
Calcium-binding is what lets **clotting proteins** and **bone proteins** work.

Adults rarely run short, because gut bacteria make some.
**Newborn babies** are the group at risk. So every baby gets a vitamin K dose at birth.

## ⭐ Learn by heart from this topic

**Three forms:**

| Form | Name | From |
|---|---|---|
| **K1** | **Phylloquinone** | green leafy vegetables |
| **K2** | **Menaquinone** | **gut bacteria** (and fermented foods) |
| **K3** | **Menadiol** (menadione) | **synthetic** |

<br>

**Job:** cofactor for **carboxylation of glutamate → Gla (γ-carboxyglutamate)**. Gla **binds calcium**.

**Two functions:** **blood clotting** (prothrombin → thrombin) and **bone formation** (osteocalcin).

**Intake:** EFSA **AI = 70 µg/day**. Rule of thumb **1 µg per kg body weight**.

**Deficiency:** Vitamin K Deficiency Bleeding (**VKDB**) in newborns.

**Toxicity:** none from food. **No UL.** But it **interferes with warfarin**.

---

## 1. Why newborns get vitamin K

Babies are born with **limited stores** of vitamin K.

Breast milk gives **too little**.

Their gut is **sterile** at birth, so no bacteria are making K2 yet.

So some babies develop **Vitamin K Deficiency Bleeding (VKDB)**, which can show **within 24 hours of birth**.
It can mean uncontrolled **internal bleeding**.

<br>

**HSE (2004):** "All babies should receive one dose of vitamin K prophylaxis at birth."

### 🧠 Check
Give three reasons newborn babies are at risk of vitamin K deficiency.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1. Limited stores at birth.
2. Low levels in breast milk.
3. Sterile gut, so no bacteria making menaquinone yet.

</details>

## 2. Structure and forms

![Forms of vitamin K](../img/03/l12-30.jpg)

**How to read this diagram:**

All three forms share the same ring at the left: the **2-methyl-1,4-naphthoquinone ring** (in red on the slide).
That ring gives **vitamin K activity**.

What differs is the side chain on the right:
- **Phylloquinone (K1)**: one long side chain. From **green leafy vegetables**.
- **Menaquinone (K2)**: a side chain of repeating units. Made by **gut microbiota**.
- **Menadiol (K3)**: no side chain. **Synthetic**.

## 3. Sources, absorption and transport

**Phylloquinone (K1)**
- Green leafy vegetables: spinach, kale, Brussels sprouts, broccoli
- Absorbed in the **small intestine** in **micelles** (about **80%**)

**Menaquinone (K2)**
- **Synthesised by intestinal bacteria**
- Absorbed by **passive diffusion** from the **ileum and colon**

**Both** forms are released from the **liver** in **VLDLs**.

<br>

**Food sources** (µg per serving):

| Food | Serving | Vitamin K (µg) |
|---|---|---|
| **Kale**, raw | 1 cup | **547** |
| Parsley, raw | ¼ cup | 246 |
| Broccoli, cooked | 1 cup | 220 |
| Spinach, raw | 1 cup | 145 |
| Green leaf lettuce | 1 cup | 62.5 |
| Olive oil | 1 tbsp | 8.1 |

Plus whatever your **intestinal bacteria** make.

## 4. How vitamin K works: carboxylation of glutamate

![Carboxylation of glutamate](../img/03/l12-35.jpg)

**How to read this diagram:**

On the left is an **inactive protein**. Green square = a **glutamic acid** residue.

A **vitamin K-dependent enzyme** adds a carboxyl group (–COOH) to it.

Now it's **Gla** (γ-carboxyglutamate), the red square. The protein is **activated**.

Gla can **bind calcium**.

In the reaction, vitamin K goes from **active to inactive**. It's then recycled back (bottom arrow).

<br>

**Why calcium binding matters:**
Calcium helps clotting proteins **stick to cell membranes** where clotting happens.
Calcium is also what **mineralises bone**.

<br>

**Prothrombin** and several other clotting proteins contain γ-carboxyglutamate.

### 🧠 Check
What does vitamin K do to glutamate, and why does it matter?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It's the cofactor for adding a carboxyl group to glutamate, making γ-carboxyglutamate (Gla).

Gla can bind calcium.

That activates proteins for blood clotting and bone formation.

</details>

## 5. Function 1: Blood clotting

![Clotting cascade](../img/03/l12-33.jpg)

**How to read this diagram:** it's the clotting cascade. Each box activates the next, like dominoes.

What to know:
1. Vitamin K **activates clotting factors** (by making Gla, §4).
2. **Prothrombin → thrombin.**
3. Thrombin turns **fibrinogen → fibrin**.
4. Fibrin threads **form the clot**.

No vitamin K = inactive clotting factors = **bleeding**.

---

## 6. Function 2: Bone formation

Two bone proteins need Gla:
- **Osteocalcin**
- **Matrix Gla protein** (bone matrix GLA)

These are **skeletal proteins** that **bind calcium**.
So vitamin K affects **bone mineralisation**.

## 7. Requirements

The requirement is **difficult to determine** (gut bacteria make an unknown amount).

So it's an **Adequate Intake**, not a PRI:

| Source | Value |
|---|---|
| EFSA AI | **70 µg/day** |
| Rule of thumb | **1 µg per kg body weight** |
| Typical range | **65–80 µg/day** |

---

### 📐 Equation 1: vitamin K by body weight

**AI (µg/day) = 1 µg × body weight (kg)**

| Symbol | Means | Units |
|---|---|---|
| AI | Adequate Intake of vitamin K | µg/day |
| body weight | the person's weight | kg |

**What it means:**
Bigger body = bigger need. A 70 kg adult needs about 70 µg/day, which matches EFSA's 70 µg.

**Use it when:**
You're given a body weight and asked for the vitamin K intake.

**Watch out:**
It's a rule of thumb for an **AI**, not a precise requirement.

### 🧠 Check
A man weighs 82 kg. Estimate his vitamin K AI. Does 1 cup of raw spinach cover it?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

AI = 1 × 82 = **82 µg/day**

1 cup of raw spinach = 145 µg. So yes, it covers it.

</details>

## 8. Measuring status

1. **Prothrombin time**: how long blood takes to clot when **calcium + thromboplastin** are added. Longer = less vitamin K.
2. **Plasma preprothrombin**: prothrombin with **little or no Gla**. If there's lots of it, the carboxylation step isn't happening.

---

## 9. Deficiency and toxicity

**Deficiency**
- **Rare in adults** (gut bacteria + leafy greens)
- Some **newborns** have low reserves (§1):
  sterile gut, low levels in breast milk
- They can develop **VKDB**, with uncontrolled internal bleeding
- Prevented by a **single dose at birth**

<br>

**Toxicity**
- **No evidence of toxicity** from food
- **No dietary upper limit**
- Toxicity is known only for the **synthetic** form (K3)
- High doses can **reduce the effectiveness of anticoagulant drugs** like **warfarin**

<br>

**Why the warfarin link makes sense:**
Warfarin works by blocking vitamin K, so blood clots less.
Eat lots of vitamin K and you undo the drug's effect.

### 🧠 Check
Someone on warfarin starts eating a big bowl of kale every day. Why might their doctor be worried?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Kale is very high in vitamin K (547 µg per cup).

High vitamin K intake can reduce the effectiveness of anticoagulants like warfarin.

So their blood could clot more than intended.

</details>
