## Part A: Core questions

### Q1: Forms of vitamin A (Core Q1)

Name the three preformed forms of vitamin A and say what chemical type each is. What is provitamin A?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Retinol: alcohol form.

Retinal (retinaldehyde): aldehyde form.

Retinoic acid: acid form.

Provitamin A = carotenoids, like β-carotene, which can be cleaved in the body to give retinaldehyde.

From: §2

</details>

### Q2: Vision (Core Q2)

Explain the role of vitamin A in vision, and why deficiency causes night blindness.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Vitamin A helps maintain the cornea, through differentiation and function of epithelial cells.

In the eye, 11-cis-retinal joins opsin to form rhodopsin.

Light flips it to all-trans-retinal, which splits from opsin. That split sends a signal to the brain.

All-trans-retinal is converted back to cis-retinal and rhodopsin is reformed, or it's converted to retinoic acid.

In deficiency, there isn't enough retinal to restore cis-retinal. So it takes longer to adapt to the dark, and seeing in poor light is impaired: night blindness.

From: §6

</details>

### Q3: Gene expression (Core Q3)

Describe how retinoic acid regulates gene expression. Give two examples of what the regulated genes encode.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1. Retinoic acid moves into the nucleus.
2. It binds to receptors (RAR and RXR) that are part of the retinoic acid response element (RARE) in the promoter region of certain genes.
3. Binding enhances transcription of those genes.
4. The effect depends on the tissue, e.g. cell differentiation in epithelial cells.

Examples (any two): structural proteins like skin keratins, enzymes like alcohol dehydrogenase, proteins for epithelial cell differentiation, immune cells.

From: §7

</details>

### Q4: Deficiency and status (Core Q4)

Describe the deficiency signs of vitamin A, and two ways status can be assessed.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Deficiency:**
Night blindness: impaired production of rhodopsin.
Xerophthalmia: dryness of the eye from inadequate mucus, Bitot's spots, progressing to irreversible blindness.
Hyperkeratosis: too much keratin, as immature skin cells fail to differentiate.
Impaired immunity.

**Status** (any two): liver biopsy (direct but invasive), dark adaptation time, Bitot's spots, Relative Dose Response test (% change in serum vitamin A after a dose).

From: §8, §9

</details>

### Q5: Calculation: RAE of a meal (Core Q5)

A meal contains 150 µg retinol, 2400 µg β-carotene and 480 µg of other carotenoids.

Find the total vitamin A in µg RAE. What % of an adult woman's 600 µg does it give?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: convert each part.**

Retinol counts 1 : 1 = 150 µg RAE

β-carotene ÷ 12 = 2400 ÷ 12 = 200 µg RAE

Other carotenoids ÷ 24 = 480 ÷ 24 = 20 µg RAE

**Step 2: add.**

RAE = 150 + 200 + 20 = **370 µg RAE**

**Step 3: % of the woman's RDA.**

370 ÷ 600 × 100 = **62%**

From: §3 Equation 1, §4

</details>

### M1: True or false

1. β-carotene is a form of preformed vitamin A.
2. Retinol is absorbed more efficiently than carotene.
3. After leaving the liver, retinol travels bound to retinol binding protein.
4. Night blindness is caused by impaired production of rhodopsin.
5. Xerophthalmia is always reversible.
6. High intakes of vitamin A in pregnancy are linked to birth defects.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1. **False.** β-carotene is provitamin A. Preformed = retinol, retinal, retinoic acid. (§2)
2. **True.** Retinol 70–90%, carotene 5–60%. (§5)
3. **True.** With transthyretin. (§5)
4. **True.** (§8)
5. **False.** It progresses to irreversible blindness. (§8)
6. **True.** Vitamin A is teratogenic; UL in pregnancy is 3000–3300 µg. (§10)

### M3: Multiple choice

Which receptor does 9-cis retinoic acid bind to?

- A. RAR
- B. RXR
- C. RARE
- D. Retinol binding protein

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

9-cis retinoic acid binds RXR. All-trans retinoic acid binds RAR. RARE is the DNA response element, not a receptor.

### M6: Explain

Why can preformed vitamin A become toxic, and why are pregnant women warned about it?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The body has limited capacity to metabolise vitamin A, so excess builds up in the liver and other tissues.

Unbound vitamin A causes liver and bone damage.

It's also teratogenic: high intakes are linked to birth defects. So the UL for pregnant women is 3000–3300 µg/day, and very rich sources like liver are a risk.

From: §10

</details>

### M7: Explain

Explain why vitamin A deficiency is a major public health concern, and describe one food-based approach to tackling it.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It's linked to more than 600,000 deaths a year among children under five, as well as night blindness, xerophthalmia and blindness, and impaired immunity.

The HarvestPlus project increases vitamin A intake by breeding staple crops richer in provitamin A, such as orange-fleshed sweet potato.

From: §1, §8

</details>
