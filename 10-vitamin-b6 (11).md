## Part A: Core questions

### Q1: Vitamers (Core Q1)

Name the three main B6 vitamers and the chemical type of each. What is the active form, and what is the inactive end product?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Pyridoxine: alcohol.

Pyridoxal: aldehyde.

Pyridoxamine: amine.

Each also exists phosphorylated, making 6 vitamers.

Active form: pyridoxal phosphate (PLP).

Inactive endpoint: 4-pyridoxic acid, which is excreted.

From: §1

</details>

### Q2: Metabolism (Core Q2)

Describe the absorption and metabolism of vitamin B6.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1. Absorbed in the small intestine in the free (dephosphorylated) form.
2. Most is taken up by the liver, where it's phosphorylated (phosphorylated forms don't cross cell membranes) and oxidised to pyridoxal phosphate.
3. PLP is exported bound to albumin.
4. Tissue concentrations are controlled by the balance between phosphorylation and dephosphorylation.
5. 80% of body B6 is in muscle as PLP associated with glycogen phosphorylase.

From: §3

</details>

---

### Q3: Functions (Core Q3)

Describe the three main areas of metabolism in which PLP is a coenzyme, with an example where possible.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1. **Amino acid reactions:**
   transamination: PLP carries the amino group (coenzyme to transaminase), transferring it to an α-keto acid; needed to make non-essential amino acids. Example: alanine + α-ketoglutarate → pyruvate + glutamate.
   decarboxylation to form amines. Example: glutamate → GABA (a neurotransmitter).
2. **Cofactor of glycogen phosphorylase**, the enzyme that breaks down glycogen.
3. **Regulation of steroid hormones:** terminates their action by removing the hormone–receptor complex from DNA binding.

From: §4

</details>

### Q4: Deficiency (Core Q4)

Why is severe B6 deficiency rare? What does moderate deficiency cause?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It's widely distributed in foods.

Moderate deficiency causes abnormalities of amino acid metabolism and sensitivity to steroid hormone action, leading to mood changes, skin and mucosal issues, mild anaemia and reduced immune function. (Summary: dermatitis, irritability, anaemia.)

From: §5

</details>

### Q5: Calculation: B6 and protein (Core Q5)

An athlete eats 140 g of protein a day.

1. Find their B6 reference intake in mg/day.
2. Find their average requirement in mg/day.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**1.**

15 × 140 = 2100 µg = **2.1 mg/day**

**2.**

13 × 140 = 1820 µg = **1.82 mg/day**

From: §2 Equation 1

</details>

### M4: Explain

Why is the B6 requirement linked to protein intake?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

PLP is the coenzyme for amino acid reactions: transamination and decarboxylation.

The more protein is eaten, the more amino acids need to be processed, so more B6 is needed.

That's why the requirement is set as 13 µg (AR) or 15 µg (reference intake) per g protein.

From: §2, §4

</details>

---

### M5: Explain

Explain why the liver phosphorylates B6, and how this controls tissue levels.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Phosphorylated forms of B6 don't cross cell membranes, so phosphorylation traps B6 inside the cell.

To move between tissues it must be dephosphorylated.

So tissue concentrations are controlled by the balance between phosphorylation and dephosphorylation.

From: §3

</details>
