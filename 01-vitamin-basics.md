## The big idea

Vitamins are **micronutrients**.
You need them in tiny amounts: **µg or mg** a day.

Most can't be made by the body.
So they have to come from the diet.

There are two families:
**fat-soluble** (A, D, E, K) and **water-soluble** (the B group and C).
Which family a vitamin is in tells you how it's absorbed, stored, and how easy it is to overdose.

## ⭐ Learn by heart from this topic

**What makes something a vitamin** (5 points):
1. Mostly **not made** in the body, so needed from the diet
2. Lack of it causes **disease**, and giving it back **cures** the disease ("vital amine")
3. No shared chemical structure
4. Gives **no energy**
5. Needed in **small amounts** (µg or mg)

<br>

**Fat-soluble = DEKA** (or "ADEK").
**Water-soluble = B group + C.**

<br>

**The three EFSA reference values:**

| Value | Covers | Memory hook |
|---|---|---|
| **AR** Average Requirement | **half** the population | the middle of the bell curve |
| **PRI** Population Reference Intake | **virtually all** people | AR plus a safety margin |
| **AI** Adequate Intake | used when there's **no AR** | "what healthy people eat seems fine" |

## 2. What micronutrients do

Seven big jobs:

1. Bone health
2. Antioxidants
3. Oxygen transport
4. Enzyme function
5. Immune function
6. Electrolytes in body fluids
7. Other tissue functions

<br>

**Bone health is the classic example of nutrients working together:**

| Nutrient | Job in bone |
|---|---|
| Phosphorus, magnesium, fluoride | structural parts of bone |
| **Vitamin D** | keeps the **calcium balance** right |
| Zinc, manganese, iron | enzymes in bone formation |
| **Vitamin K** | enzymes in bone formation |
| Copper, **vitamin C** | enzymes in bone formation (**collagen**) |

So three vitamins show up in bone: **D, K and C**.

## 3. What makes a vitamin a vitamin

The word comes from "**vital amine**".
Early on, people thought they were all amines. They aren't, but the name stuck.

<br>

The five points:

**1. Mostly not made in the body.**
So they must come from food.
(Exceptions you'll meet: vitamin D from sunlight on skin, vitamin K2 and biotin from gut bacteria, niacin from tryptophan.)

**2. Deficiency causes disease. Repletion cures it.**
Take the vitamin away and a specific disease appears.
Give it back and the disease goes.

**3. No common chemical structure.**
They're grouped by what they do for us, not by what they look like.

**4. Don't yield energy.**
They help **release** energy from carbs, fat and protein (the B vitamins especially).
But they don't supply any calories themselves.

**5. Small amounts.**
µg (micrograms) or mg (milligrams) a day.
1 mg = 1000 µg.

### 🧠 Check
"B vitamins give you energy." True or false?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

False.

Vitamins don't yield energy.

B vitamins are coenzymes that help the body **release** energy from food. The energy comes from the carbs, fat and protein.

</details>

## 4. Fat-soluble vs water-soluble

This table is a classic exam question. Learn it row by row.

| | Water-soluble (B, C) | Fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) |
|---|---|---|
| **Absorption** | directly into the **blood** | into the **lymph**, then blood |
| **Transport** | travel **freely** | many need **carrier proteins** |
| **Storage** | **circulate**, not stored | **stored in fat tissue** (and liver) |
| **Excretion** | kidneys remove excess in **urine** | less easily excreted, stay in fat |
| **Toxicity** | **possible** at very high doses | **likely** at very high doses |
| **Requirements** | needed **more often** | needed **less often** (stores) |

<br>

**Why it all hangs together:**

Fat-soluble vitamins travel with fat.
So they're absorbed with dietary fat, packed into **chylomicrons**, and go into the **lymph** first.
They get **stored** in the liver and fat tissue.
Stores mean you don't need them every day.
But stores also mean they can **build up** to toxic levels.

Water-soluble vitamins dissolve in blood.
Extra is filtered out by the kidneys and lost in urine.
So you need them **more often**, and toxicity is less likely.

<br>

**Memory hook:** fat-soluble vitamins **stay** (stored, toxic). Water-soluble vitamins **wash out** (urine, needed often).

### 🧠 Check
Why are fat-soluble vitamins more likely to reach toxic levels?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

They're stored in fat tissue and the liver.

They're not easily excreted.

So with very high doses they build up.

Water-soluble vitamins are mostly removed by the kidneys in urine.

</details>

## 5. Dietary Reference Values (EFSA)

These are the numbers that set how much of a nutrient people should eat.

<br>

**Average Requirement (AR)**
The intake that's adequate for **half** of the people in a group.
It assumes requirements follow a **normal distribution** (a bell curve).

**Population Reference Intake (PRI)**
The intake that's adequate for **virtually all** people in a group.
It sits higher than the AR, out in the tail of the bell curve.

**Adequate Intake (AI)**
Used when a PRI **can't** be set, because there isn't enough data to find an AR.
It's the average intake of a **healthy** population, assumed to be enough.

<br>

**In the lectures:**
- PRI is given for thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, B6, folate.
- AI is given for vitamin K, vitamin E, pantothenic acid and biotin (not enough data for an AR).
- Older Irish (FSAI) tables also use "RDA". Treat it like a PRI.

### 🧠 Check
Biotin has an "AI" and not a "PRI". What does that tell you?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

There wasn't enough data to set an Average Requirement.

So EFSA used the average intake of a healthy population instead and assumed it was adequate.

</details>

## 6. How recommendations changed over time

The timeline slide shows how nutrition advice evolved, from about 1900 to the 2010s:

1. Vitamins discovered (early 1900s)
2. Nutrient requirements set, then RDAs (around WW2)
3. "Optimal": dietary guidelines for **optimal** health, not just avoiding deficiency
4. Food-based dietary guidelines (FBDGs), then changes in the terms used
5. Safe Upper Limits (**UL**), then functional foods
6. Health claim legislation (most recent)

<br>

The big shift: from "**how much stops a deficiency disease**" to "**how much is optimal, and how much is too much**".

You don't need to learn dates. Know the direction of travel.
