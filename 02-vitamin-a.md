## The big idea

Vitamin A comes in two kinds:
**preformed** vitamin A (retinol, from animal foods) and **provitamin A** (carotenoids like β-carotene, from orange and green plants).

Its big jobs are **vision**, **gene expression** (growth, cell differentiation, reproduction) and **immune function**.

Lack of it is a huge world problem: night blindness, then blindness, then death in young children.

Too much (preformed) is toxic, because it's **fat-soluble and stored in the liver**.

## 1. Why vitamin A matters

Vitamin A deficiency is a **major public health concern**.

More than **600,000 deaths a year** among children under five are linked to it.

## 2. Forms of vitamin A

![Forms of vitamin A](../img/02/l12-13.jpg)

**How to read this diagram:**

The top three structures are **preformed** vitamin A. Only the end group (red box) changes:
- **Retinol**: ends in –CH₂OH, the **alcohol** form
- **Retinaldehyde (retinal)**: ends in –CHO, the **aldehyde** form
- **Retinoic acid**: ends in –COO⁻, the **acid** form. It comes as all-trans and 9-cis.

The bottom two are **provitamin A** carotenoids.
**β-carotene** is a long symmetrical molecule. It's **cleaved** in the middle to give retinaldehyde.

<br>

**Where they come from:**
Preformed vitamin A: animal foods (liver, milk, eggs).
Provitamin A: plants (sweet potato, carrots, spinach, red peppers, mango).

## 3. Carotenoids and RAE

![Carotene to retinol](../img/02/l12-14.jpg)

**How to read this diagram:**

β-carotene (top) is split in half by **carotene dioxygenase** (red box).

That gives **2 retinaldehyde**.

Retinaldehyde can go two ways:
- to **retinol** (reversible, retinol dehydrogenase)
- to **all-trans retinoic acid** (one-way, aldehyde oxidase)

<br>

There are **50+ carotenoids** in food. The main provitamin A ones: **α-, β-, γ-carotene and cryptoxanthin**.

<br>

### 📐 Equation 1: Retinol Activity Equivalents (RAE)

**1 µg RAE = 1 µg retinol = 12 µg β-carotene = 24 µg other carotenoids**

So for a mixed food or meal:

**RAE (µg) = µg retinol + (µg β-carotene ÷ 12) + (µg other carotenoids ÷ 24)**

| Symbol | Means | Units |
|---|---|---|
| RAE | Retinol Activity Equivalents: total vitamin A activity | µg |
| retinol | preformed vitamin A in the food | µg |
| β-carotene | provitamin A, counts **1/12** | µg |
| other carotenoids | α-carotene, cryptoxanthin etc., count **1/24** | µg |

**What it means:**
Carotenes are much **less active** than retinol, so you need lots more of them to get the same effect.

**Use it when:**
You're asked how much vitamin A a food or diet gives, or to compare a plant food with an animal food.

**Does NOT apply to:**
Preformed retinol. That counts 1 : 1.

<br>

**Why carotenes count for so little** (4 reasons):
1. **Poor absorption** of carotene
2. **Poor intestinal activity** of carotene dioxygenase
3. Other carotenoids can **inhibit** carotene dioxygenase
4. Sometimes cleavage happens in **the wrong place**, giving 8-, 10- or 12-apo-carotenals. These are oxidised to **retinoic acid, not retinol**.

### 🧠 Check
A carrot portion has 3600 µg β-carotene. How many µg RAE is that?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

RAE = 3600 ÷ 12 = **300 µg RAE**

That's about half a woman's daily 600 µg.

</details>

## 5. Absorption, transport and storage

![Vitamin A metabolism](../img/02/l12-18.jpg)

**How to read this diagram:** start at the gut (bottom) and follow the arrows.

1. In the gut, **retinol esters are hydrolysed by lipases**.
2. Absorption: **retinol 70–90%** efficient. **Carotene only 5–60%**.
3. In the gut cell, retinol is **re-esterified** and packed into **chylomicrons**.
4. Chylomicrons go into circulation and take vitamin A to the **liver**.
5. The liver either:
   - **stores** it in lipid droplets (main store: **liver**, also adipose), or
   - **releases** it bound to **retinol binding protein (RBP)** and **transthyretin**.

<br>

This is the fat-soluble pattern from Topic 1: absorbed with fat, chylomicrons, stored in liver, needs a carrier protein.

### 🧠 Check
Name the two proteins that carry retinol in the blood after it leaves the liver.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Retinol binding protein (RBP) and transthyretin.

</details>

## 6. Function 1: Vision

Four functions overall: **vision, growth, reproduction, immune function**.

<br>

Vitamin A helps vision in two ways:

**a. The cornea.**
It keeps the cornea healthy, through the **differentiation and function of epithelial cells**.

**b. Turning light into a nerve signal.**

![The visual cycle](../img/02/l12-21.jpg)

**How to read this diagram:** the box on the right is a rod cell in the eye.

1. **11-cis-retinal** joins **opsin** to make **rhodopsin**.
2. **Light** hits rhodopsin.
3. The retinal flips shape to **all-trans-retinal**. It splits off from opsin.
4. That split sends a **signal to the brain** → vision.
5. all-trans-retinal is turned back into **11-cis-retinal** (via retinol, in the pigment layer on the left), and rhodopsin is **reformed**.
   Some is lost instead, converted to retinoic acid.

<br>

**Why deficiency causes night blindness:**
Every flash of light uses up some retinal.
Without extra vitamin A, there isn't enough retinal to **restore cis-retinal** and rebuild rhodopsin.
So it takes longer to **adapt to darkness**, and seeing in poor light is impaired.

### 🧠 Check
Light hits rhodopsin. What happens to the retinal, and what does that cause?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

11-cis-retinal flips to all-trans-retinal and splits from opsin.

The split sends a nerve signal to the brain, which is vision.

</details>

## 7. Function 2: Gene expression

![Vitamin A and gene expression](../img/02/l12-23.jpg)

**How to read this diagram:** the oval is the cell nucleus. The wiggly line is DNA.

1. **Retinoic acid** moves into the **nucleus**.
2. It binds to **receptors**:
   - all-trans retinoic acid → **RAR** (retinoic acid receptor, α/β/γ)
   - 9-cis retinoic acid → **RXR** (retinoic X receptor, α/β/γ)
3. The receptors sit on the **RARE** (retinoic acid response element), in the **promoter region** of certain genes.
4. Binding **enhances transcription** of those genes.
5. What happens depends on the tissue. In **epithelial cells**, it drives **cell differentiation**.

<br>

**Genes retinoic acid switches on encode:**
- structural proteins (e.g. **skin keratins**)
- enzymes (e.g. **alcohol dehydrogenase**)
- proteins for **cell differentiation** (e.g. epithelial cells)
- **immune cells**

That's why vitamin A matters for **embryonic development** and **immune function** too.

<br>

**Carotenoids** have their own extra job: they act as **antioxidants**.

### 🧠 Check
Which form of vitamin A works in the nucleus, and what does it bind to?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Retinoic acid.

It binds to RAR and RXR receptors on the RARE in the promoter region of genes, and enhances their transcription.

</details>

## 8. Deficiency

**1. Night blindness**
Not enough rhodopsin is made. Slow to adapt to the dark.

**2. Xerophthalmia** ("dry eye")
The eye dries out because there's **not enough mucus**.
**Bitot's spots** appear: small white patches of dead cells on the eye.
It progresses to **irreversible blindness**.

**3. Hyperkeratosis** (too much keratin)
Immature skin cells fail to **differentiate** properly.
(Gene expression, §7, goes wrong.)

**4. Impaired immunity.**

<br>

**Memory hook:** night blindness is **reversible**. Xerophthalmia ends **irreversible**.

## 9. Measuring status

1. **Liver biopsy**: the direct measure (the liver is the store), but **invasive**.
2. **Dark adaptation time**: how fast you adapt to dim light.
3. **Bitot's spots**: visible dead cells on the eye surface.
4. **Relative Dose Response (RDR) test**: give a dose of vitamin A, then measure the **% change** in serum vitamin A.
   If liver stores are low, the serum level jumps a lot.

### 🧠 Check
Why isn't liver biopsy used routinely, even though it's the direct measure?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It's invasive.

So easier indirect tests are used: dark adaptation time, Bitot's spots, or the Relative Dose Response test.

</details>

## 10. Toxicity

- The body has a **limited capacity to metabolise** vitamin A.
- Excess builds up in the **liver** and other tissues.
- **Unbound** vitamin A (not on RBP) causes **liver and bone damage**.
- So there are recommended **upper limits**.
- It's **teratogenic**: high intakes in pregnancy are linked to **birth defects**.
  **UL for pregnant women = 3000–3300 µg/day.**

<br>

So pregnant women are warned off **liver** and high-dose supplements (one portion of liver is over 6,500 µg, §4).

Toxicity comes from **preformed** vitamin A. Carotenes are converted slowly (§3), so they don't cause it in the same way.
