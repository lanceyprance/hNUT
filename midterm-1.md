<details><summary>Q2 answers</summary>

**a.** Any four, 2 marks each:
Absorption: water-soluble directly into blood; fat-soluble via lymph, then blood.
Transport: water-soluble travel freely; many fat-soluble need carrier proteins.
Storage: water-soluble circulate; fat-soluble stored in fat tissue and liver.
Excretion: water-soluble excess removed by kidneys in urine; fat-soluble less readily excreted.
Toxicity: possible vs likely at very high doses.
Requirements: water-soluble needed more often; fat-soluble less often.
→ Topic 1 §4

**b.** Fat-soluble vitamins are stored and not readily excreted, so high intakes build up.
Vitamin A: limited capacity to metabolise it, builds up in the liver, unbound vitamin A damages liver and bone, teratogenic; UL in pregnancy 3000–3300 µg/day.
Or vitamin D: raises blood calcium, raised blood pressure, soft tissue calcification; threshold unknown, risk above 250 µg/day in adults and 50 µg/day in infants; US UL 100 µg.
→ Topic 2 §10, Topic 4 §11

</details>

<details><summary>Q3 answers</summary>

**a.** Skin: UVB converts 7-dehydrocholesterol → previtamin D3 → vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol). Carried on DBP.
Liver: 25-hydroxylase → 25-hydroxyvitamin D (calcidiol).
Kidney: 1-hydroxylase → 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), the active form.
(Dietary D2 and D3 join via chylomicrons; D2 follows the same steps.)
→ Topic 4 §4

**b.** Low blood calcium → PTH secreted → PTH increases 1-hydroxylase activity → more active vitamin D. 1,25(OH)₂D inhibits its own synthesis and increases 24-hydroxylase, making 24-hydroxycalcidiol (negative feedback).
→ Topic 4 §4

**c.** Increases intestinal calcium absorption; increases reabsorption in the kidney; increases mobilisation of bone mineral.
→ Topic 4 §7

**d.** 6.7% below 30 nmol/L, 21.9% below 40, 40.1% below 50, 75.6% below 75. Below 50 nmol/L: 55% in winter vs 28.9% in summer. Ireland is above 40° N, so winter UVB is too weak for skin synthesis; 25(OH)D peaks in late summer and falls through winter. (Cut-offs are debated.)
→ Topic 4 §5, §6

</details>

<details><summary>Q4 answers</summary>

**a.** Thiamin: TDP (TPP). Riboflavin: FAD (and FMN). Niacin: NAD (and NADP). Pantothenic acid: coenzyme A.
→ Topic 6 §2, Topic 7 §1, Topic 8 §2, Topic 9 §2

**b.** Pyruvate dehydrogenase does oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate:
Thiamin (TDP): coenzyme for the decarboxylation.
Riboflavin (FAD): electron carrier, becomes FADH₂.
Niacin (NAD⁺): electron acceptor, becomes NADH, which feeds the ETC.
Pantothenic acid (CoA): accepts the 2-carbon acetyl group, forming acetyl-CoA, which enters the TCA cycle.
→ Topic 6 §4, Topic 9 §4

**c.** NAD⁺ is an electron acceptor in oxidative, energy-yielding reactions (glycolysis, pyruvate → acetyl-CoA, β-oxidation, TCA); NADH takes electrons to the ETC for ATP. NADPH is the major coenzyme for reductive biosynthesis, e.g. fatty acid synthesis and folate metabolism. NAD breaks down; NADPH builds.
→ Topic 8 §4

**d.** Beriberi = chronic thiamin deficiency with high carbohydrate intake.
Dry (nervous system): weakness, stiffness and cramps in the legs; muscle wasting and paralysis spreading upward.
Wet (heart): dilated arterioles, fast blood flow and pulse, heart failure and oedema.
Alcoholics: low thiamin intake, high carbohydrate intake, impaired absorption, liver damage → Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome.
→ Topic 6 §6, §7

</details>

<details><summary>Q5 answers</summary>

**a.** Folate carries single carbons. 5,10-methylene-THF is needed to convert dUMP → dTMP (thymidylate) for DNA; folate is also needed to make methionine for DNA methylation. Rapidly dividing cells need lots of thymidine. Without folate, DNA synthesis fails and division slows, but RNA synthesis continues, so cells grow large: big (macrocytic), irregular, often nucleated red cells = megaloblastic anaemia. Bone marrow, intestine and hair follicles are also affected.
→ Topic 12 §6, §8

**b.** Anencephaly: the upper end of the neural tube fails to close; the brain fails to develop. Spina bifida: incomplete closure of the spinal cord; mild or severe.
→ Topic 12 §9

**c.** (i) 400 µg folic acid daily before conception and for the first 12 weeks of pregnancy. (ii) 4 mg (4000 µg) daily, from at least 1 month before conception and for the first 4 months of pregnancy.
→ Topic 12 §10

**d.** Intake had already increased through voluntary fortification; there was some evidence for negative health effects.
→ Topic 12 §11

</details>
