## Part A: Core questions

### Q1: Forms and sources (Core Q1)

Name the three forms of vitamin K, and give the source of each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

K1, phylloquinone: green leafy vegetables (kale, spinach, Brussels sprouts).

K2, menaquinone: synthesised by intestinal bacteria (and fermented foods).

K3, menadiol: the synthetic form.

### Q2: Carboxylation (Core Q2)

Explain how vitamin K activates proteins, and why that's needed for blood clotting and bone.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Vitamin K is a cofactor for a vitamin K-dependent enzyme that carboxylates glutamate residues in certain proteins.

This forms γ-carboxyglutamate (Gla), which activates the protein.

Gla can bind calcium.

In clotting: prothrombin and other clotting proteins contain Gla, and calcium binding lets them work (prothrombin → thrombin, fibrinogen → fibrin, clot).

In bone: osteocalcin and matrix Gla protein bind calcium, which affects bone mineralisation.

From: §4, §5, §6

</details>

### Q3: Newborns (Core Q3)

Why are newborn babies at risk of vitamin K deficiency, and what is done about it in Ireland?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

They have limited stores at birth, breast milk contains low levels, and their gut is sterile so no bacteria are making menaquinone.

Some develop Vitamin K Deficiency Bleeding (VKDB), within 24 hours of birth, which can mean uncontrolled internal bleeding.

HSE (2004): all babies should receive one dose of vitamin K prophylaxis at birth.

From: §1, §9

</details>

---

### Q4: Status and toxicity (Core Q4)

How is vitamin K status measured? Is there a toxicity risk?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Status:** prothrombin time (clotting time with calcium + thromboplastin added) and plasma preprothrombin (prothrombin with little or no Gla).

**Toxicity:** no evidence of toxicity from food, so there's no dietary upper limit. Toxicity is known only for the synthetic form. But high doses can reduce the effectiveness of anticoagulant drugs like warfarin.

From: §8, §9

</details>

2. Phylloquinone is absorbed in the colon by passive diffusion.
3. Vitamin K deficiency is common in healthy adults.
4. Both forms of vitamin K are released from the liver in VLDL.

3. **False.** It's rare in adults. Newborns are the risk group. (§9)
4. **True.** (§3)
5. **False.** No evidence of toxicity, so no UL. (§9)
6. **True.** Osteocalcin and matrix Gla protein. (§6)

- A. Phosphorylates them
- B. Carboxylates them to form Gla
- C. Methylates them
- D. Removes them

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

Vitamin K is the cofactor for carboxylation of glutamate to γ-carboxyglutamate (Gla), which binds calcium.

### M5: Explain

A patient on warfarin is told to keep their intake of green leafy vegetables steady and not suddenly increase it. Explain why.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Green leafy vegetables are rich in vitamin K (phylloquinone).

Vitamin K activates clotting factors. High intakes can reduce the effectiveness of anticoagulant drugs like warfarin.

A sudden rise in vitamin K could make the drug work less well, so blood would clot more than intended.

From: §3, §9

</details>

### M6: Explain

Outline the steps in blood clotting that depend on vitamin K.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Vitamin K carboxylates glutamate in clotting factors to Gla, which activates them by letting them bind calcium.

Activated factors convert prothrombin to thrombin.

Thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin.

Fibrin forms the clot.

From: §4, §5

</details>
